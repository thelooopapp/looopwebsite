Before You Link Mom's Bank Account to Any App, Ask These 5 Questions

By the Looop team·October 6, 2026·6 min read

"Would we even know if Mom got scammed?"

It's a question a lot of families are asking. More apps now offer to help, by watching for unusual spending on a parent's bank account and letting the family know. That can be genuinely useful. It also means handing over some of the most personal data a person has.

So before you link Mom's bank account to any app, including ours, it's worth asking a few plain questions. Here are five, with what a good answer sounds like.

Why families are thinking about this

Scams that target older adults are a real and growing problem. The Federal Trade Commission reports that fraud losses reported by adults 60 and over rose about fourfold, from about $600 million in 2020 to $2.4 billion in 2024. The FTC says the rise was driven largely by reports of losses over $100,000, often from investment scams, romance scams and impersonators.

Many of those losses show up first as money leaving an account. That's why a heads-up about unusual spending can help a family notice sooner, and why apps are starting to offer it.

The data that makes it work is sensitive, though. A bank account shows where someone shops, what they give to, which doctors they pay and how they live. It deserves at least as much care as a location.

Question 1: Does this company sell data or work with advertisers?

This is the first question for a reason. If an app's business includes selling data or advertising, then data about your family is part of how it makes money. That's true even if the company never sells bank data specifically.

What to look for: a plain sentence, like "We never sell your data and we don't work with advertisers." Be careful with softer wording such as "we don't sell personal information," which can come with exceptions in the fine print.

Where to check: the privacy policy, the App Store privacy label and the company's own news. Some family location apps have sold location data in the past, and some run growing ad businesses today. Our guides on how location apps make money and who's paying for your family safety app explain what to look for.

None of this means an app will misuse spending data. It means you should know the business model before you decide.

Question 2: How does the connection work?

Most apps don't connect to a bank directly. They use a service that sits in between. Plaid is one of the best known.

With Plaid, the person signs in to their own bank through Plaid's screen. Plaid then retrieves the account information the person has approved and shares it with the app. Plaid says it doesn't sell or rent people's data to outside companies.

What to look for: the app names the service it uses, and you can see who's connecting to what. Plaid offers a free Plaid Portal where people can see which apps they've connected and stop access.

Question 3: What will the app see, and what will it actually do?

"Link your account" can mean very different things. Ask which data the app reads, how often, what it keeps, and what it does with it.

Just as important: ask what the app doesn't promise. No app can tell whether a payment is a scam. It can only notice that something looks different. Be wary of any app that says it will "stop" or "prevent" fraud on its own.

What to look for: a clear, specific description of the feature, plus honest limits.

Question 4: Did Mom say yes, and does she know what's shared?

This one matters most. A bank account belongs to the person whose name is on it. Linking it should be her decision, made with her, not for her.

Sit down together. Show her what the alert looks like and who will get it. Let her ask questions, and take "not right now" for an answer.

A tool your parent agreed to is one she'll keep. A tool set up behind her back breaks the trust you're trying to protect. Our guide on how to check on aging parents without making them feel watched has ideas for starting that conversation.

What to look for: an app that expects consent and makes it easy to see who receives alerts.

Question 5: How do you turn it off, and delete it?

Plans change. Mom might change her mind. You might switch apps. Before you link anything, find out how to undo it.

What to look for: a simple way to unlink each account inside the app, a way to delete your account and data, and the option to stop access from the connection service too, such as through Plaid Portal.

Other ways to help, with or without an app

An app can be one helpful piece. These steps work on their own, too.

  • Ask her bank about a trusted contact. Federal banking agencies have encouraged banks to offer a trusted contact process, so they know who to reach if something seems wrong.
  • Turn on the bank's own alerts. Many banks can text or email when a large payment goes out. Mom can choose who gets them.
  • Talk about common scam signs. The FTC lists four signs: someone pretending to be from an organization you know, a problem or prize, pressure to act right away, and a demand to pay in a specific way, like gift cards, wire transfers or crypto.
  • Agree on a "call me first" rule. The FTC's best advice is to stop and talk to someone you trust before acting. Make that person easy to reach.

How Looop answers these questions

Looop is a safety notifications app. Spending alerts are one optional piece of it. Here's how we answer each question.

  1. Data and advertisers: Looop never sells your data and doesn't work with advertisers. Not bank data, not location data, not anything.
  2. The connection: Bank linking runs through Plaid. Looop never sees bank passwords.
  3. What it does: Looop reads transactions on a linked account and sends a heads-up when one goes over an amount you set. An unusually large payment can be an early sign of a scam, so you can call Mom that same day. Looop can't tell whether a payment is a scam, and it doesn't stop or reverse payments. It just tells you when a call might be worth making.
  4. Consent: Loved Ones join Looop by invitation. We encourage families to set up spending alerts together, with the account holder's OK.
  5. Turning it off: Bank linking is optional. Skip it and everything else in Looop still works. Unlink any account in the app, and delete your account and data whenever you choose.
