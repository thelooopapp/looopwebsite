The Holiday Visit Checklist: What to Notice When You See Your Parents

By the Looop Team·October 17, 2026·7 min read

"Mom sounds fine on the phone. But is she really?"

For a lot of families, the holidays are the one time of year everyone is under the same roof. You'll eat too much pie, argue about the thermostat, and laugh at the same old stories. You'll also get something a phone call can't give you: a real look at how your parents are doing.

The National Institute on Aging says the best way to know what someone needs is to ask them directly. But a phone call or text isn't always enough to tell, and some parents don't want to cause worry.

This checklist is a gentle guide to what to notice this Thanksgiving or holiday season. Most of what you see will be reassuring. If something does catch your eye, treat it as a reason to talk with them.

Before you go: a few ground rules

  • You're a guest. Your parent's home is their home. Notice quietly. Don't open drawers or take notes at the table.
  • Look for change. A messy kitchen during holiday cooking means nothing. A kitchen that looks very different from last year might.
  • One thing is not a pattern. The NIA's guidance on memory notes that it's normal to forget things once in a while at any age. Look for patterns over several days.
  • Enjoy the visit. The point of the holidays is being together. Let this list sit in the background.

Around the house

The NIA suggests a few simple questions when you spend time in a parent's home: can they cook safely, is the home fairly clean and uncluttered, and do they have the medicines they need? (NIA)

  • The kitchen and fridge look about as stocked as usual, with fresh food that isn't long expired
  • The stove and oven are being used safely
  • The home is about as tidy as you'd expect from them
  • Walkways are clear, with no loose rugs or cords to trip on
  • Stairs have sturdy railings, and rooms and hallways are well lit
  • The bathroom has grab bars where they'd help, near the tub, shower, or toilet
  • Smoke detectors work, and the heat is on and comfortable

The CDC's home safety tips for older adults include getting rid of tripping hazards, adding grab bars, putting railings on both sides of stairs, and adding brighter light bulbs. (CDC) These are easy holiday-weekend projects to offer help with.

Health, fitness, and everyday care

  • They seem steady on their feet, and getting around the house as easily as last year
  • They're still getting out for walks, or the exercise they enjoy, like a class or the gym
  • Their weight looks about the same, without big gains or losses
  • They're keeping up with bathing and wearing clothes that suit the weather
  • Their medicines are organized and refilled, and they seem to be taking them
  • Glasses, hearing aids, or dentures are in use and working well
  • They've seen their doctor recently, or have an appointment coming up

The NIA lists significant weight change, poor hygiene, confusion, and trouble walking among the signs that someone may need extra support. (NIA)

Memory and mood

  • They follow conversations and remember recent plans and news
  • They're not asking the same question over and over
  • Familiar tasks, like a favorite recipe, still go smoothly
  • Their mood seems like their usual self over a few days, at dinner and away from the table
  • They still enjoy the things they love

The NIA points out that a parent with depression might brighten up for a short call or visit, but serious mood problems are harder to hide during a longer stay. (NIA) It also lists signs that memory changes may be more than normal aging, like getting lost in familiar places or having trouble following directions. (NIA)

If your parent ever says they feel hopeless or you're worried they might harm themselves, call or text the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline at 988, as the NIA advises.

Money and mail

  • Mail isn't piling up unopened
  • Bills look paid, with no stack of past-due notices
  • No unusual new "friends," prize letters, or urgent calls asking for money
  • They seem comfortable when money comes up

The NIA's elder abuse guidance lists sudden, unexpected financial losses or unpaid bills, despite having enough money, among the signs worth paying attention to. (NIA) If something looks off, raise it gently and privately.

Driving

  • The car has no new dents or scrapes they can't explain
  • They seem relaxed behind the wheel, if you ride along
  • No recent tickets, near misses, or comments from neighbors
  • They're comfortable driving at night, or have found other ways to get around

These come from the NIA's list of signs a loved one may need to change their driving. (NIA) The NIA also reminds families that for many people, giving up the keys feels like losing freedom. Keep the conversation on skills and safety.

Phone and technology

  • Their phone is charged, and they can hear it ring
  • They know how to make a video call, if they'd like to
  • Their emergency contacts are saved and up to date
  • They're not being bothered by strange calls, texts, or pop-ups

This is a great time to help with small tech chores they've been meaning to get to. Ask first, and let them drive.

Friends and connection

  • They still see friends, neighbors, or their faith community
  • They have regular plans that get them out of the house
  • Someone nearby could stop by if needed, and you have that person's number

Staying connected matters. The NIA notes that loneliness and social isolation are associated with higher risks for health problems such as heart disease, depression, and cognitive decline. (NIA) The NIA also points out that you can live alone without feeling lonely. Plenty of parents living on their own have the fullest calendars in the family.

How to raise a concern, kindly

If you noticed something, wait for a good moment to bring it up.

Choose a quiet time. A walk, a drive, or a cup of coffee the next morning gives you privacy, away from the holiday table and the rest of the family.

Lead with care. The NIA suggests naming your worry without sounding critical, for example asking whether it's been hard to get to the store when the fridge looks bare. (NIA)

Start with "I." "I worry about the drive home in the dark" lands better than "You shouldn't be driving at night." The NIA recommends this approach for driving talks. (NIA)

Ask what they want. AARP's caregiving advice is to remember your parent is still in charge, and to ask, "Would you like my help?" (AARP)

Offer something specific. Groceries delivered, a ride to an appointment, a new night-light. Practical help is easier to say yes to.

Bring in siblings, gently. If you share the load, talk with your brother or sister before you leave so nobody is caught off guard and your calls to Dad don't overlap.

What to set up before you leave

The drive home is when the worry usually kicks in. A little setup before you go can make the next few months calmer for everyone.

  • A shared contact list. Doctors, pharmacy, a trusted neighbor, and who has a spare key, shared with siblings.
  • The paperwork conversation. AARP suggests learning whether your parent has advance directives and powers of attorney in place. (AARP) A calm holiday visit is a good time to ask.
  • A neighbor or friend on call. With your parent's permission, the NIA suggests asking people who see them regularly to call you with any concerns. (NIA)
  • A plan for regular calls. Keep them about everyday life and what's new with them.
  • A quiet heads-up for the in-between days. That's where Looop can help, if your parent agrees.

Your visit, step by step

  • Use the holiday visit to notice things gently.
  • Look for changes from last year, and patterns over a few days.
  • Check the home, health, mood, money, driving, technology, and friendships.
  • Raise concerns privately, with care, and ask what they want.
  • Set up a few simple supports before you leave, with your parent's say-so.

Most of all, enjoy the visit, and save this list for a quiet moment. You'll have plenty of time to think it over on the drive home.
