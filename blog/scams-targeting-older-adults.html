Scams That Target Older Adults: Signs Families Can Notice Early

By the Looop team·October 6, 2026·8 min read

"Dad, who was that on the phone?"

Most families never expect to ask that question with real worry. Then a parent mentions a call from "the bank," or a new online friend, or a computer warning that wouldn't go away. And you start to wonder.

Scams targeting older adults are not a sign that anyone is careless or confused. They are run by organized groups who are good at pressure, fear and urgency. The best defense is often simple: people who talk to each other, early.

This guide covers what the latest federal data shows, the most common scam types, warning signs families can notice, how to bring up the topic without talking down to a parent, and what to do if a scam has already happened.

What the numbers say

Two federal agencies publish the most widely used data on fraud. Both point the same way.

  • FBI, 2025. People 60 and older filed 201,266 complaints with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) in 2025 and reported $7.748 billion in losses, up 59% from 2024. The average reported loss was $38,500, and 12,444 people in this age group reported losing more than $100,000. (FBI IC3 2025 Annual Report, Elder Fraud section)
  • FTC, 2024. Reported fraud losses from people 60 and older totaled nearly $2.4 billion in 2024, up from about $600 million in 2020. People 80 and older had a median reported loss above $1,600. (FTC, Protecting Older Consumers 2024-2025, December 2025)
  • Most fraud is never reported. Because so many cases go unreported, the FTC estimates the true cost of fraud to older adults in 2024 was between $10.1 billion and $81.5 billion, depending on the method used. (Same FTC report)

For all ages, the FTC says people reported losing $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025, from 3 million fraud reports (FTC, March 25, 2026).

One more finding is worth knowing. The FTC reports that older adults actually report losing money to fraud at lower rates than younger adults. But when they do lose money, the amounts tend to be larger. That's why an early conversation matters so much.

The scams that come up most

Scammers change their stories often, but the patterns repeat. Here are the ones federal data shows most often for people 60 and older.

Scam type How it usually starts 2025 losses reported to IC3 by people 60+
Investment A friendly contact online or on social media, often pitching cryptocurrency $3,519,296,354
Tech or customer support A pop-up, call or email saying a computer or account has a problem $1,040,730,043
Romance or confidence A new online relationship that slowly turns to money $584,032,745
Government impersonation A caller claiming to be from a federal agency, often with a threat $413,206,251
Lottery, sweepstakes or inheritance A message saying you've won, but must pay fees first $136,328,519

Source: FBI IC3 2025 Annual Report, crime types reported by complainants 60 and older.

By number of complaints, phishing and spoofing (fake emails, texts and caller IDs) led the list, with 48,064 complaints from people 60 and older in 2025. Tech and customer support scams were second.

The FTC sees a related pattern. Older adults were much more likely than younger adults to report losing money to tech support scams, prize and lottery scams, romance scams and government impersonation scams. Business impersonators, especially fake bank callers, were a major source of losses. These scams often claim an account is at risk and urge the person to move money to "keep it safe." (FTC, December 2025)

How the money moves

How a scammer asks to be paid is often the clearest clue.

According to the same FTC report, older adults reported the largest total losses on scams paid by bank transfer, followed by cryptocurrency. Gift cards were the most common payment method in several scam types, including government impersonation, tech support, romance and family impersonation scams.

A simple rule helps everyone in the family: no real bank, government agency or tech company will ask you to pay with gift cards, cryptocurrency, a wire to a stranger, or cash handed to a courier.

Warning signs families can notice

You don't need to be nearby to notice that something is off. Many signs show up in ordinary conversations.

In how they talk

  • They mention a new friend online they haven't met in person, who has started talking about money or a hard-luck story.
  • They're suddenly secretive about phone calls, or say someone told them not to tell family.
  • They sound anxious about a problem with their computer, their bank account, their Social Security or a "warrant."
  • They mention a prize, an inheritance or an investment that sounds unusually good.

In what they do

  • They ask how to buy gift cards, use a cryptocurrency machine or send a wire.
  • They let someone they don't know connect to their computer remotely.
  • They're rushing, often because someone told them they had to act today.

In their money

  • Large or unusual payments, withdrawals or transfers.
  • New payees they can't easily explain.
  • Bills going unpaid when there was plenty of money before.

None of these signs proves a scam. Lots of large payments are perfectly normal. A sign is just a reason to ask, kindly.

How to talk with a parent without talking down

Your parent has handled money, work and family for decades. Start there.

Lead with respect, not rescue. Try: "I keep reading how convincing these scam calls have gotten. They're going after everyone. Have you gotten any weird ones?" This puts you on the same side, against the scammers.

Share, don't lecture. Tell them about a scam text you got last week. Scams target every age, and saying so makes the conversation feel shared.

Agree on a family rule ahead of time. For example: "If anyone ever tells you to keep something secret from us, or to pay with gift cards or crypto, call me first. No questions, no judgment." Agreeing now is easier than arguing later.

Set up a family code word. If someone calls claiming to be a grandchild in trouble, your parent can ask for the code word. A real family member will know it.

Keep shame out of it. If your parent has already sent money, they may feel embarrassed. Scammers count on that. Say clearly: "This happens to smart, careful people. I'm glad you told me. Let's sort it out together."

Respect their choices. Offering help is not the same as taking over. Ask what they'd like support with, and let them lead. Our guide on checking on aging parents without making them feel watched has more on this.

What to do if a scam happens

Speed matters. The FBI's Recovery Asset Team works to freeze funds when complaints are filed quickly. In 2025 it reported freezing $32,865,655 tied to incidents involving victims 60 and older (FBI IC3 2025 Annual Report). Here's what to do, in order.

  1. Stop contact. Hang up, don't reply and don't send more money, even if the scammer threatens or promises a refund.
  2. Call the bank or card company right away. Use the number on the back of the card or on a statement, not one the caller gave you. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment and to flag the account.
  3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  4. Report it to the FBI at ic3.gov, especially for online, wire or cryptocurrency losses.
  5. If personal information was shared, go to IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan.
  6. Get free, friendly support. The AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline is free and open to anyone, at 877-908-3360, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. Eastern. The U.S. Department of Justice also runs a free national fraud hotline for people 60 and older at 833-FRAUD-11 (833-372-8311), Monday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern.
  7. Watch for follow-up scams. People who lost money are sometimes contacted by fake "recovery" services. Real government agencies don't charge to get money back.

If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

The bottom line

Scams work by isolating people and rushing them. Families work best by staying close and talking early. You don't need to watch over a parent's every purchase. You need a warm, shame-free habit of asking, and a plan for what to do if the answer is "Actually, something strange did happen."
