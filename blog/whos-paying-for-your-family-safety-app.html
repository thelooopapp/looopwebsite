Who's Really Paying for Your Free Family Safety App?

By the Looop team·October 6, 2026·6 min read

"If this app is free, how does it make money?"

It's a fair question to ask about any app. It matters more for a family safety app, because of what it knows. A location-sharing app can learn where your family lives, works, shops, worships and spends the night. That's some of the most personal information there is.

This guide explains the common ways location-sharing apps make money, walks through one well-known example using public sources, and ends with a simple checklist for choosing a family safety app that doesn't sell your data.

Facts in this article are drawn from the public sources linked throughout, current as of October 5, 2026.

Why this matters more for family apps

Most apps know some things about you. A family location-sharing app can know a lot, about several people at once.

Think about what a pattern of places can reveal. A regular Tuesday visit to a clinic. A Sunday morning at a place of worship. A child's school. A parent's new apartment after a separation. Even without names attached, a pattern like that can say a great deal about a person.

And with a family app, it isn't only your information. It's your kids', your parents', your partner's. Many of them joined because you asked them to.

So it's worth knowing who else might be paying for the app, and what they're getting for it.

Three common ways location-sharing apps make money

Not every app does all of these. Many do at least one. Here's what each looks like in plain terms.

1. Advertising inside the app

The simplest model is ads. The app shows you offers, banners or sponsored content, and advertisers pay to reach you.

What makes location-based ads different is timing and context. An app that knows where you are can show an offer based on a real place you just visited. For an advertiser, that's valuable. For a family, it can mean your safety app is also a marketing channel.

2. Partner offers in your notifications

A step further is placing partner offers in push notifications. These arrive on your lock screen, the same place you'd see an alert about a loved one.

That blurs a line many families care about. A safety app's notifications should mean "pay attention." When some of them are ads, the signal gets noisier.

3. Selling or sharing location data

The model that worries privacy advocates most is selling location data to third parties, often called data brokers. Brokers buy data from many sources and resell it, or insights from it, to other businesses.

Apps that do this often describe the data as "aggregated" or "de-identified." Those terms can mean different things in different contexts, and it's worth reading an app's privacy policy closely to see exactly what's shared and with whom.

A case study: Life360

Life360 is one of the best-known family location-sharing apps, and it's a useful example because much of its business model is described in its own press releases, its filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, and reporting from journalists. Here is what those public sources say.

What it is

Life360 is a GPS location tracking app built around a live family map and location history. Its notifications focus on arrivals, departures and missed arrivals at set places. Its own setup guide cites location accuracy to about 50 feet.

An advertising platform

Life360 launched an advertising platform with Uber as a brand partner. According to the company, members who land at an airport can receive an Uber ride offer as a push notification. In an investor release, Life360 reported that 34 million of these were sent.

In June 2025, Life360 launched Place Ads, which target families based on the real-world places they visit.

Ads beyond the app

Life360 bought the ad platform Nativo to serve ads using its first-party family data across a publisher network. In an SEC filing, it reported nearly $20 million in ad revenue in Q1 2026, up over 300% year over year.

Adweek reported on a LiveRamp partnership that lets advertisers target Life360 users off-site, meaning outside the Life360 app itself.

Data sales

Life360 has a history of selling location data to a dozen data brokers. In 2021, The Markup reported that Life360 sold precise location data to brokers including Cuebiq, X-Mode, SafeGraph and Allstate's Arity.

Life360 then stopped most of those sales, but kept Arity and began selling aggregated data to Placer.ai, which it describes as a growing data business. In short, Life360 still sells location data today.

What to take from this

None of this is hidden. Life360 describes much of it publicly, and many families use the app knowing how it works. The point isn't that any one family made the wrong choice. It's that "free" usually means someone else is paying, and families deserve to know who.

If an app's business includes advertising built on location data, or selling location data, that's worth weighing alongside its features.

Questions to ask any family safety app

Before you invite your family, take ten minutes with the app's privacy policy and website. Look for clear answers to these questions.

  • Does it sell data? Look for a plain "we never sell your data," not "we don't sell personal information" with exceptions in the fine print.
  • Does it work with advertisers? Check for ad partners, sponsored offers, or an ad platform described in company news.
  • Are there ads in notifications? If partner offers can arrive as push notifications, your alerts may be mixed with marketing.
  • What does it collect, and why? A good app uses only what it needs to do its job.
  • Is health sharing optional? If the app can show steps, sleep or heart rate, each person should choose whether to share.
  • Can people leave easily? Anyone in your group should be able to leave, and you should be able to delete your account and data whenever you choose.
  • Does it require consent? Every person should join by invitation and know what's shared.

If the answers are vague, that tells you something, too.

What to look for: a checklist

Here's the short version. A family safety app worth trusting should be able to check every box.

  • Never sells your data
  • Doesn't work with advertisers
  • No partner offers or ads in your notifications
  • Uses only what it needs to tell when something seems off
  • Every person joins by invitation
  • Health and wellness sharing is optional
  • Anyone can leave a group anytime
  • You can delete your account and data whenever you choose
  • A clear, plain-language privacy policy

The bottom line

Every app has a business model. With a family safety app, it's worth knowing exactly what it is, because the information involved belongs to the people you love.

Ask the questions. Read the policy. Choose an app whose business depends on helping your family, not on what your family's data is worth to someone else.
