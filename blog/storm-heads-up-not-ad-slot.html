A Storm Near Mom Is a Heads-Up, Not an Ad Slot

By the Looop team·October 6, 2026·6 min read

"Is that storm going to hit Mom's town?"

If your parent lives hours away, you know the feeling. You see the forecast, or a news alert, and suddenly you're refreshing a radar map for a place you can't drive to tonight.

A weather alert about someone you love has one job: to tell you it might be a good time to call. This guide covers what to do when a storm is headed toward a parent who lives far away, and why we think that alert should never be anything else.

Watch, warning or advisory: what the words mean

Before you pick up the phone, it helps to know how serious the alert is. The National Weather Service uses three main terms. (NWS)

Term What it means What to do from afar
Advisory A weather threat that's more of a nuisance than a danger, like slick roads from light snow A quick text is usually plenty
Watch Conditions could produce a certain kind of severe weather. It isn't expected yet Call to talk through the plan while there's time
Warning Life-threatening weather is expected. Act now Keep it short. Make sure they're taking shelter or following local instructions

The difference matters. A watch is your window to help them get ready. A warning is the moment for one short call, not a long one.

What to do when a storm is headed toward a parent

You can't board up their windows from 800 miles away. But you can help in real ways. Here's a simple plan.

1. Call during the watch, not the warning

The best time to talk is before the weather arrives. Phones work, power is on, and nobody is rushed. Keep it calm and practical: "I saw the forecast for your area. Want to walk through the plan together?"

Respect that they've likely seen storms before. You're offering a second set of eyes, not taking over.

2. Talk through four questions

Keep the call short. These four questions cover most of what matters:

  • Where will you go if it gets bad? Sheltering from a tornado looks different from leaving early for a hurricane or flood. Whatever the storm, local officials' instructions come first.
  • Do you have what you need for a few days? Ready.gov suggests one gallon of water per person per day for several days, plus a several-day supply of non-perishable food, a flashlight, extra batteries and a battery or hand crank radio.
  • Are your medicines covered? Ready.gov's guidance for older adults suggests a kit with medicines, medical supplies, batteries and chargers.
  • Is your phone charged? A full phone and a charged backup battery can be the difference between a quick check-in and hours of not knowing.

3. Agree on how you'll check in

Decide together, before the storm, how you'll reach each other. For example: "I'll text you at 8 tonight. If the power's out, just send a thumbs-up when you can."

A plan like this means less worrying for you and fewer calls for them. If you don't hear back right away, try not to assume the worst. A quiet phone often just means a low battery or spotty service.

4. Share the load with family

If you have siblings or other relatives, split the job. One person calls Mom. Another checks with a neighbor. Someone else watches for updates from local officials. Agree on who does what, so Mom gets one call, not five.

5. Know who's close by

Ready.gov encourages people to build a support network of family, friends and others who can help in an emergency. For a parent who lives alone, that might be a neighbor, a friend from church or a nearby relative.

Have at least one local number saved before the storm. If you can't reach your parent after it passes, a neighbor who can knock on the door is often the fastest way to know they're okay.

6. If the power goes out

Power outages are one of the most common effects of a big storm. Two safety points are worth saying out loud:

  • Generators belong outside. The CDC says never to use a generator inside a home or garage, even with doors and windows open, and to keep it more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.
  • Plan for anything that needs power. If your parent relies on a medical device that plugs in, talk through a backup plan with them and their care team ahead of time.

7. Follow local officials

If local officials tell your parent's area to evacuate, encourage them to go. You can help from afar by looking up the route, finding a place to stay, or calling a relative they could stay with.

And if you believe someone is in immediate danger, call 911. An app is never a substitute for emergency services.

8. After the storm passes

Check in once the weather clears. Ask about power, water, roads and any damage. Offer to help with the follow-up calls, like the insurance company or a repair service, if they'd like that.

What a weather alert should feel like

A good weather alert is calm, clear and about one person: someone you love. It says a storm is heading their way. It gives you time to call. Then it gets out of the way.

That's why we think the alert itself should never be selling anything.

Not every app sees it that way. Life360 offers severe weather alerts for its members through a partnership with AccuWeather. In the September 2025 announcement, Life360 also said it will use AccuWeather's data to "more effectively target advertising during weather events," with offers from brands that help members stock up, prepare or recover.

Families can decide how they feel about that. For us, a storm near Mom is a moment to reach out. It's not an ad slot.
