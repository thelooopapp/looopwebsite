The Long-Distance Caregiving Report: What the Research Says (2026)

By the Looop team·October 6, 2026·9 min read

"Is Dad okay today, or am I just worried because I'm far away?"

If you care for someone from another city or state, you already know that question. What you might not know is how many people share it. We gathered the most reliable long distance caregiving statistics we could find, from AARP, the National Alliance for Caregiving, Pew Research Center, the National Institutes of Health and the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and put them in one place.

Looop hasn't run its own survey yet (more on that at the end). So this is a research roundup: what existing, reputable studies say about the people who care from a distance, and what families can take from it.

The short version

  • 63 million American adults were family caregivers in 2025, roughly one in four adults. (AARP and NAC, 2025)
  • 11% of caregivers of adults live an hour or more away from the person they care for. (AARP and NAC, 2025)
  • 54% of Americans in their 40s are in the "sandwich generation." (Pew Research Center, 2025 survey)
  • 26% of U.S. adults ages 65 and older lived alone in 2023. (Pew Research Center analysis of Census data)
  • 75% of adults 50 and older want to stay in their current homes as they age. (AARP, 2024)

How many people are caregivers?

A lot, and the number keeps growing.

In 2025, 63 million American adults provided ongoing care to an adult or child with a medical condition or disability, according to Caregiving in the US 2025 from AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving. That's roughly one in four adults (24 percent), and a 45 percent increase since the survey was fielded in 2015. Of those 63 million, 59 million care for an adult.

The average caregiver in that study is 51 years old, and most are caring for a relative, especially a parent or spouse.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counts it a different way. It found that 38.2 million people provided unpaid eldercare in 2023 to 2024, with people ages 55 to 64 the most likely to do so (24 percent).

Pew Research Center asked people directly in a September 2025 survey. Ten percent of all U.S. adults said they are a caregiver for a parent age 65 or older. Among adults who have a parent that age, the share rises to 24 percent.

Different methods, same picture: caring for a parent is an ordinary part of adult life for tens of millions of people.

What counts as long-distance caregiving?

The National Institute on Aging uses a simple rule. If you live an hour or more away from someone who needs care, you're a long-distance caregiver.

By that measure, long-distance caregivers are a real and sizable group. In Caregiving in the US 2025, more than 10 percent of caregivers of adults live an hour or more away from the person they care for. Here is how the full picture breaks down.

Where the caregiver lives (2025) Share of caregivers of adults
In the same household 40%
Within 20 minutes 35%
20 minutes to 1 hour away 13%
1 to 2 hours away 4%
2 or more hours away 8%

Source: AARP and National Alliance for Caregiving, Caregiving in the US 2025, Figure 8. The report totals the last two rows as 11% one hour or more away.

Living apart changes the shape of care. The same report found that caregivers who live apart from the person they care for are more likely to have help, both unpaid (65 percent, compared with 36 percent of those living together) and paid (39 percent, compared with 22 percent). Long-distance care often means coordinating a team, not doing every task yourself.

That matches what the NIH describes: arranging care, helping with bills and paperwork, talking with health care providers, and being an important source of emotional support, all from afar.

The sandwich generation

Many long-distance caregivers are also raising kids or helping adult children get on their feet.

According to Pew Research Center, a quarter of U.S. adults (25 percent) were in the "sandwich generation" in its September 2025 survey. Pew defines that as having a parent age 65 or older plus a child under 18, an adult child they've supported financially, or both.

The share peaks in midlife:

  • 54% of adults in their 40s
  • 45% of adults in their 50s, up from 36% in 2021
  • 25% of adults in their 30s

Caregiving in the US 2025 saw the same pattern from the caregiver side. Nearly one-third of caregivers care for both an adult and a child under 18 at home, and that rises to 47 percent among caregivers under 50.

If you're the person in the middle, with kids in one place and a parent in another, you're in good company.

More parents living on their own

Living alone is normal, and for many people it's a choice they love. It also means fewer people around to notice a rough day.

A Pew Research Center analysis of Census Bureau data found that 26% of U.S. adults ages 65 and older lived alone in 2023, down slightly from 29% in 1990. The share is higher for some groups:

  • 31% of women 65 and older lived alone, compared with 19% of men.
  • 38% of adults 85 and older lived alone, compared with 24% of those ages 65 to 84.

And among care recipients in Caregiving in the US 2025 who live apart from their caregiver in a home or community setting, 3 in 10 live alone.

Time, money and work

Caregiving takes real hours and real dollars, whether you're down the street or across the country.

Time. Caregivers in Caregiving in the US 2025 spend an average of 27 hours per week providing care. Thirty percent have been caregiving for five years or more.

Work. Seventy percent of working-age caregivers (ages 18 to 64) are employed. Half of working caregivers have had to arrive late, leave early or take time off to provide care, and 18 percent moved from full-time to part-time or cut their hours. Only 49 percent say their supervisor knows they're a caregiver. (AARP and NAC, 2025)

Money. Nearly half of caregivers in the 2025 report have had at least one negative financial impact. One-third have stopped saving, and 24 percent have used up short-term savings. An earlier AARP study from 2021 found that 78% of people caring for an adult family member had regular out-of-pocket costs, averaging $7,242 a year, or 26% of the caregiver's income on average.

Pew's caregivers felt it too. Among people who regularly help an aging parent, more said the effect on their job or career was negative (30%) than positive (17%), among those who are employed. Their financial situation followed the same pattern: 32% negative, 18% positive.

The emotional side: stress and worry

The numbers on time and money are easier to count. The worry is harder, but researchers have tried.

  • In Caregiving in the US 2025, nearly two-thirds of caregivers (64 percent) report moderate or high emotional stress.
  • 24 percent say they feel alone, up since 2020.
  • Three in 10 caregivers want more help or information to manage their emotional or physical stress, and 27 percent want help keeping the person they care for safe at home.
  • In Pew's survey, people who regularly help an aging parent were more likely to say it has hurt their emotional well-being (39%) than helped it (28%). Women were more likely than men to say so (47% vs. 30%).

There's a brighter side, too. In that same Pew survey, 56% of these caregivers said helping their parent has had a positive impact on their relationship with that parent. And in the AARP and NAC report, more than half of caregivers say the role gives them a sense of purpose.

Worry and closeness often show up together. That's worth remembering on the hard days.

How caregivers use technology

Caregivers are leaning on technology more than ever, especially to stay connected across distance.

According to Caregiving in the US 2025:

  • 25% of caregivers have used an app, video, a wearable or a similar tool to check on the person they care for from a distance, up from 13% in 2020. The report notes this has nearly doubled.
  • 41% use technology or software to keep up with the person's health records, up from 30% in 2020.
  • Younger caregivers are more likely than caregivers over 50 to use these remote check-in tools (30% vs. 22%).

The report says these tools help caregivers stay organized when they can't be there in person.

What older adults say they want

Here is the part families sometimes forget: most parents want to stay right where they are.

AARP's 2024 Home and Community Preferences Survey found that 75% of adults 50 and older wish to remain in their current homes as they age, and 73% hope to stay in their communities. Over half (51%) say they need a home that supports independent aging.

In other words, the goal for most families isn't to move Mom closer or to manage Dad's day. It's to help them keep living their way, with the right support around them.

What this means for families

Research can't tell you what your family needs. But a few practical takeaways stand out.

1. You're not alone in this. Millions of people care for someone they don't live with. If you feel stretched, that's a common and reasonable response to a real load, not a personal failing.

2. Build a team, not a to-do list. Caregivers who live apart are more likely to have paid and unpaid help. Map out who is nearby: a sibling, a neighbor, a friend from church, a local care manager. Agree on who calls when.

3. Get the paperwork in one place. NIH News in Health suggests gathering important papers somewhere easy to reach and making sure at least one caregiver has written permission to receive medical and financial information.

4. Talk with your employer. With fewer than half of working caregivers saying their supervisor knows, a simple conversation may open up flexible hours or leave you didn't know you had.

5. Protect your own well-being. Nearly two-thirds of caregivers report moderate or high emotional stress. Respite care, a support group or one honest conversation with a friend all count.

6. Respect their independence. Most adults 50 and older want to stay in their homes. Ask what support your parent actually wants before you set anything up, and let them lead.

7. Use technology that reduces worry, not adds to it. Pick tools your parent agrees to, that show only what helps, and that stay quiet when all is well.
