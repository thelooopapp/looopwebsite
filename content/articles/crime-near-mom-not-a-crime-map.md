# Crime Near Mom, Not a Crime Map

"Is that anywhere near Mom's place?"

Maybe you saw a headline from her city. Maybe a friend mentioned something on social media. Either way, you're 800 miles away, opening a map, zooming in, and trying to guess whether a red dot is two blocks from her house or two miles.

That moment is why we built crime alerts near family the way we did. Not as a crime map to scroll, but as a calm heads-up about crime reported near someone you love, so you know when a call is worth making.

This guide covers why a heads-up works better than a map for most families, how to choose an alert distance, what to do when an alert arrives, and where Looop's crime alerts do and don't work.

## Why a map makes worry bigger

Crime maps are useful tools for some jobs. If you're choosing an apartment or a city council is planning patrols, seeing every incident in one place helps.

For a family caring from a distance, a map often does the opposite of what you want.

- **It shows everything, everywhere.** A city map fills up with incidents that have nothing to do with the person you love. Every dot asks for your attention.
- **It turns you into the analyst.** You end up working out distances, reading report codes and guessing what "disturbance" means.
- **It invites checking.** Once you open a map, it's easy to open it again tomorrow. And the day after. That isn't peace of mind. It's homework.
- **It doesn't tell you what to do.** A map shows you what happened. It doesn't say whether this is a moment to reach out.

What most families actually want is narrower: tell me if something happened near Mom, and otherwise let me get on with my day.

## What a heads-up does instead

Looop's Nearby Crime Alerts work from the other direction. Instead of you searching a city for trouble, Looop speaks up only when crime is reported near a Loved One, within the distance you chose.

- **Quiet by default.** No alert means nothing was reported nearby. No news really is good news.
- **About one person, not a whole city.** Each alert is tied to someone you care about.
- **A nudge, not a prediction.** An alert doesn't mean your loved one is involved or in danger. It means something happened nearby, and it might be a good time to check in.
- **Their day stays theirs.** Mom doesn't have to report in or open an app. She lives her day. You hear about it only when it's worth a call.

There is a family map in Looop for the moment after an alert, if you want more context. But the map is a supporting feature. The point is the heads-up.

## How to choose your alert distance

In Looop, you choose the crime alert distance for each Loved One: 1, 3, 5 or 10 miles. There's no single right answer. A few questions help.

**How dense is their area?** In a busy city neighborhood, a lot happens within a few miles. A 1-mile distance keeps alerts close to home. In a small town or a rural county, a wider distance may make more sense, since fewer incidents are reported and they're spread out.

**What would you actually do with the alert?** If you'd only call about something on Mom's street, start small. If you'd want to know about something near the grocery store she drives to every Tuesday, go wider.

**How much do you want to hear?** More distance means more alerts. If you find yourself getting heads-ups you wouldn't act on, shrink the distance. If you feel like you're missing things, widen it.

**Is this person in a new place?** A college student in their first semester, or a parent who just moved, might warrant a different setting than someone who's lived on the same block for 30 years.

| Distance | Often a good fit for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 mile | Busy city neighborhoods, or when you only want to hear about things very close to home |
| 3 miles | Suburbs and mid-size cities, or a campus and the streets around it |
| 5 miles | Spread-out suburbs, or someone whose errands cover a wider area |
| 10 miles | Small towns and rural areas where incidents are fewer and farther apart |

Start with a guess. Adjust after a couple of weeks. You can change it anytime.

## What to do when an alert arrives

A crime alert can make your heart jump, especially when it's about a parent or a kid. Here is a calm way to handle it.

**1. Take a breath.** Most alerts are about something that happened nearby, not to your loved one. Reported crime in a neighborhood is common. The alert is information, not an emergency.

**2. Reach out simply.** Call, or send a short text. Keep it light: "Saw there was something going on near you. Everything okay over there?" In Looop, you can call, text or FaceTime straight from their card, or send a one-tap "Are you ok?"

**3. Don't lead with the scary part.** You don't need to read them the details. A warm check-in feels like care. A list of what happened can feel like alarm.

**4. Let them set the tone.** If they're fine, they're fine. Say "Good, love you" and let it go. If they want to talk about it, listen.

**5. Coordinate if you share the load.** If siblings or friends get the same alert, agree on who calls. One call feels like love. Five calls feel like a crisis.

**6. If you can't reach them and something feels wrong, trust your gut.** Try another way, like a neighbor, a friend nearby or an emergency contact. If you think someone is in danger, call 911 first.

Looop helps you know when to check in. It doesn't prevent crime, and it isn't an emergency service.

## Talk about it before you turn it on

Crime alerts work best when the person they're about knows they exist.

Before you set anything up, tell your parent, your college student or your friend what you'll see and why. A simple version: "If something gets reported near your place, I'll get a heads-up, so I might call. That's it. I won't be calling every day."

## Where crime alerts work, and where they don't

Being clear about limits matters as much as the feature itself.

- **Within 25 miles of a supported area.** If a Loved One is within 25 miles of a supported city or county, alerts can work for them, even in a nearby town that isn't on the list itself.
- **Not everywhere.** If your loved one lives far from any supported area, Looop won't send crime alerts about them. Severe weather alerts and other nudges still work.
- **Coverage changes.** We review coverage regularly, and the list may change.
- **Reported crime only.** Alerts reflect what has been reported. Not every incident is reported, and reports can take time.

## The bottom line

You don't need to know everything that happens in Mom's city. You need to know when something happened near Mom, so you can call and hear her say she's fine.

That's the difference between a crime map and a heads-up. One asks you to keep watching. The other lets you stop.
